Governments publish their law online, and a growing amount of public and private activity is built directly on those publications: compliance systems, regulatory technology, and models grounded in statutory text. All of it assumes the official record is available when needed and internally consistent when read. That assumption is rarely tested, because testing it requires a contemporaneous account of what each attempt to retrieve the official text returned. This article reports one. In building a corpus of Saudi Arabian legislation, every verified article was annotated with the sources consulted and how they were reconciled, and every instrument was assigned a verification tier by hand. Across the 11,704 articles whose record names its sources, the official source was unreachable for 20.0 per cent, covering 28.0 per cent of instruments; 20.9 per cent were obtained through a web archive, two thirds of those cases coinciding with a live source that would not respond; 8.2 per cent required optical reconstruction; and 4.9 per cent record a defect in the official text itself. Between a sixth and a quarter of the corpus, depending on the unit counted, rests on evidence weaker than a cross-verified official primary. In four instruments the official record contradicts itself: the provision the portal displays and the amendment log the same portal publishes do not agree. Three conclusions follow for publishers and users of official data. Web archives are load-bearing infrastructure for public-record research and hold that position with no mandate and no guarantee of permanence. Availability and internal consistency are distinct properties needing separate measurement. And a public dataset should carry the strength of the evidence behind each record, for which we propose a minimal five-field schema derived from what this corpus recorded in free text — and from the 24 per cent of its own provenance values that turned out not to be provenance at all.
